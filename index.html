N° M-01 — Mind · Michael Sugrue

Marcus Aurelius' Meditations: The Stoic Ideal

Core ideas

  • Meditations was Marcus Aurelius' private journal, written to train his own mind while running an empire.
  • Stoicism separates what is in your control (judgment, action) from what is not (outcomes, other people).
  • Duty, reason and acceptance of impermanence are the backbone of a good life.

Most people meet Stoicism through quotes on a gym wall, which is a shame, because Meditations reads nothing like a quote book. It reads like a man arguing with himself at the end of a long day. Marcus Aurelius was the most powerful person in the Roman world, and he still had to remind himself, over and over, not to get irritated at people who were rude, slow or ungrateful. That repetition is the most honest part of the book. He never got it solved. He just kept practicing.

The split between what you control and what you don't sounds obvious until you try to live by it for one afternoon. A client goes quiet. A package shows up late. Someone misreads your message and gets short with you. In every case the outcome sits outside your reach, but the story you tell yourself about it is yours, and so is your next move. Marcus kept a ledger of that line. Judgment and action on one side, everything else on the other. The work is putting each new event in the right column before you react to it.

What makes this useful outside of philosophy class is how much of ordinary work is reacting. People who handle pressure well are rarely calmer by nature. They have simply rehearsed the response before the pressure shows up. Writing things down, the way Marcus did, is a cheap way to do that rehearsal. You catch the same complaint showing up three nights in a row and you start to see it for what it is.

Impermanence is the part people skip, and it might be the most practical. Everything you are stressed about has an expiration date, and so does everything you are proud of. Keeping both in view makes you harder to rattle and harder to flatter, which is a decent combination for anyone who deals with other people for a living. -MILLZ

Why it matters

I come back to this when I catch myself reacting to things I can't change. If an emperor could journal his way back to composure, I can too.

N° M-02 — Mind · The Diary Of A CEO

Andrew Huberman: You Must Control Your Dopamine! The Shocking Truth Behind Cold Showers!

Core ideas

  • Dopamine drives motivation and pursuit, not just pleasure.
  • Constant easy hits (phones, junk, stimulation) raise your baseline and make normal life feel flat.
  • Deliberate discomfort like cold exposure can raise dopamine in a slower, sustained way.

The common idea of dopamine is that it is the pleasure chemical, the reward you get after the fact. The more useful picture is that it is the chemical of wanting and chasing. It is what gets you out of the chair. That shift matters, because if dopamine is about pursuit, then the way you spend it shapes how much drive you have left for the things that take effort.

Phones are the obvious example. Every swipe offers a small chance of something new, and the brain treats that chance as worth chasing. Do that a few hundred times a day and ordinary work starts to feel dull by comparison. Nothing is wrong with the work. The bar for what feels interesting has just been raised by things that cost nothing. The same thing happens with junk food, background noise and constant messaging. None of it is dramatic. It just adds up.

Cold exposure gets attention because it flips the pattern. Instead of an easy spike followed by a dip, a cold shower is unpleasant up front and leaves a longer, steadier lift. Whether or not you ever step into an ice bath, the principle carries over. Hard things first, easy things later, tends to leave you with more drive at the end of the day than the reverse.

There is also a lesson here about how attention gets captured in general. Anything built to be checked often is borrowing from the same system. Knowing that makes you a sharper consumer, and it makes you more careful about what you build and put in front of other people. Respecting someone's attention is a good starting point for earning it. -MILLZ

Why it matters

This reframed discipline for me as chemistry I can manage, not willpower I either have or don't.

N° M-03 — Mind · Jordan B Peterson

You Can Make the Choice To Aim Up

Core ideas

  • Peterson argues meaning comes from voluntarily aiming at the highest good you can conceive.
  • Taking on responsibility is what gives a life weight and direction.
  • Aim shapes perception: you see what you are aiming at.

Peterson's argument is simple to say and hard to do. Pick the highest good you can honestly picture and point yourself at it, on purpose, even if you are starting from a bad spot. The aim does not have to be grand. It just has to be better than drifting. Most people never make the choice explicitly, so they end up aimed at whatever is loudest around them.

The part about perception is the piece I find most practical. What you are aiming at decides what you notice. Someone looking for a new job sees openings everywhere. Someone looking for reasons to quit sees those instead. The world did not change between those two people. Their target did. That means choosing a target is not just motivational talk, it changes the information you get every day.

Responsibility is the other half. Taking on weight, whether that is a family, a business or a promise, gives your days a shape they would not have otherwise. People tend to treat responsibility as a cost. Peterson treats it as the thing that makes a life feel like it counts, and I think anyone who has carried something real for a while knows that is true.

None of this requires being in a good position first. That is the point of calling it a choice. You can aim up from a bad week, a small apartment or a dead-end shift. The aim comes first and the circumstances follow it, slowly, if you keep at it. -MILLZ

Why it matters

Aiming up is a choice I can make every morning, regardless of where I'm starting from.

N° B-03 — Body · Goobie and Doobie

Help Your Body Heal Ep. 2 Part Two - How To Safely Decrease Excess Sodium Stores In Your Body

Core ideas

  • Excess sodium affects fluid balance and blood pressure.
  • Potassium-rich whole foods, hydration and cutting processed foods help the body rebalance.
  • Changes should be gradual and done with medical guidance if you have a condition.

Salt is one of those things nobody thinks about until a doctor brings it up. Sodium plays a real role in how the body holds water and manages blood pressure, and most people get far more of it than they realize, mostly from packaged and restaurant food rather than the shaker on the table. The body is good at adjusting, but it needs the right inputs to do it.

The approach here is refreshingly unglamorous. Eat more potassium-rich whole foods like vegetables, beans, potatoes and fruit. Drink enough water. Cut back on the processed stuff where most of the sodium hides. None of it is a secret, and that is sort of the point. The basics work when people actually do them for long enough to matter.

The note about going slowly and checking with a doctor if you have a condition is worth taking seriously. Big sudden changes to diet can cause their own problems, especially for anyone on blood pressure medication. Gradual changes are easier to keep anyway. You are more likely to stick with swapping one meal a day than overhauling your whole kitchen in a weekend.

I like content like this because it treats health as maintenance rather than a project with a finish line. Reading labels for a few weeks teaches you more about where your food comes from than most diet books, and that habit pays off long after the specific advice fades. -MILLZ

Why it matters

Caring for the body starts with basics like this. Small, boring, consistent changes beat dramatic ones.

N° B-04 — Body · Goobie and Doobie

Help Your Body Heal Ep. 1 - Plants Meat And Carbs | My Momma Ma Momma Said Carbs Are The Devil!

Core ideas

  • Plants, meat and carbohydrates all have a place in a balanced diet.
  • Demonizing a single macronutrient usually backfires.
  • Food quality and processing matter more than labels.

Every few years a new diet picks a villain. Fat, then carbs, then meat, then plants, depending on who is selling the book. Each camp has a few good points and a lot of overreach. The message of this episode is that plants, meat and carbohydrates all have a place, and that the fight over which one is evil mostly distracts from what actually matters.

What actually matters is quality and processing. A potato and a bag of chips are both carbs, but they do very different things to you. A home-cooked piece of chicken and a fast food nugget are both meat. Labels like low-carb or plant-based tell you almost nothing on their own. How close the food is to its original form tells you a lot more.

Cutting out a whole food group often backfires for a simple reason. It is hard to keep up, and when people fall off, they tend to fall off hard. A balanced plate is boring to market but easy to repeat, and repetition is where results come from. The best diet for most people is the one they can still be eating a year from now.

There is a broader lesson in how quickly people adopt a side and defend it. Food tribes work a lot like any other tribe. Once someone identifies with a camp, evidence starts to matter less than loyalty. Paying attention to your own body and adjusting based on what you see is slower, but it is a lot more honest. -MILLZ

Why it matters

I like this because it cuts through diet tribalism. Eat real food, pay attention, adjust.

N° S-01 — Soul · Manly Hall Society

Manly P. Hall: Are We Ready for a Peaceful World?

Core ideas

  • Manly P. Hall taught that outer peace depends on inner maturity.
  • Societies reflect the character of the people in them.
  • Wisdom traditions are practical tools for becoming more peaceful people.

Manly P. Hall spent most of the twentieth century lecturing on old philosophy and religion to anyone who would show up, and one theme kept coming back. A peaceful world cannot be built out of people who are at war with themselves. He was not being poetic. He meant that institutions, laws and treaties are only as steady as the people running them.

That idea scales down well. A team with one person who is constantly anxious or resentful tends to feel tense no matter how good the plan is. A household runs on the mood of whoever is most unsettled. Societies are the same thing at a larger size. Their character is assembled from millions of individual characters, and you cannot fix the sum without touching the parts.

Hall treated the wisdom traditions as tools rather than museum pieces. Stoicism, Buddhism, Christian mysticism and the rest were, to him, collections of practical methods for becoming calmer, more honest and less reactive. You did not need to believe every word to use the methods. You needed to practice them.

It is an uncomfortable message in a way, because it removes the option of waiting for the world to calm down first. If you want less conflict around you, the most direct lever you have is how you behave in the conflicts you are already part of. That is slow work, and it is also the only kind that has ever reliably worked. -MILLZ

Why it matters

It's a reminder that I can't ask the world for something I haven't built in myself first.

N° S-02 — Soul · William Donahue

359 Meditation

Core ideas

  • Meditation is training attention and awareness.
  • Stillness lets you observe thoughts instead of being run by them.
  • Regular practice matters more than long sessions.

Meditation gets sold as relaxation, but at its core it is attention training. You sit, you pick something to focus on, usually the breath, and you notice every time your mind wanders off. Then you bring it back. That is the whole exercise. The relaxation is a side effect of getting better at it.

What changes over time is the gap between a thought showing up and you acting on it. Without practice, a worry arrives and you are already halfway down the path before you notice. With practice, you catch it earlier. You see the thought as a thought, something passing through, rather than an order you have to follow. That small gap is where most good decisions get made.

Consistency beats length. Ten minutes every day does more than an hour once a week, the same way short daily workouts tend to beat a single heroic session. The brain responds to repetition. It is also far easier to protect ten minutes than to find an empty hour.

The practical payoff shows up in places that have nothing to do with sitting still. Conversations where you would normally interrupt. Emails you would normally answer too fast. Moments where a small irritation would normally turn into a bad afternoon. Training attention for a few minutes in the morning changes how the rest of the day gets spent. -MILLZ

Why it matters

Ten quiet minutes changes how I handle the next ten hours.

N° M-04 — Mind · Iman Gadzhi

Success Is Not Freedom. Here's Why.

Core ideas

  • Iman Gadzhi distinguishes financial success from true freedom.
  • Freedom has dimensions: money, time and location.
  • Building a business that needs you every hour can become another job.

A lot of people chase money assuming freedom comes attached. Iman Gadzhi's point is that it often does not. You can earn well and still have no control over your time, or be tied to one city, or run a business that falls apart the moment you stop answering messages. That is success by one measure and a trap by another.

Breaking freedom into money, time and location makes it easier to see what you are actually building. Some jobs pay well but own your calendar. Some businesses make good money but only while you are personally doing the work. Some setups give you location freedom but very little income. Most people only track the first number and are surprised when the other two never show up.

The fix is thinking in systems instead of hours. A business that depends on you for every task is really a job you created for yourself, often with worse benefits. Documenting how things are done, automating the repetitive parts and handing off what you can are the steps that turn effort into something that keeps working when you step away.

There is nothing wrong with trading time for money, especially early on. Everyone starts there. The useful habit is asking, every so often, whether the thing you are building is buying you more freedom over time or just a bigger version of the same schedule. -MILLZ

Why it matters

This is exactly why I'm building systems, not just income. I want leverage, not a new cage.

N° S-03 — Soul · The Modern Hermeticist

On Good and Evil Spirits (The Magus, 1801)

Core ideas

  • The Magus (1801) by Francis Barrett is a classic compilation of Western occult thought.
  • It frames spiritual influences as good or harmful, and stresses the practitioner's moral state.
  • It's best read as a historical window into how people mapped the unseen.

Francis Barrett's The Magus came out in 1801 and gathered a large amount of older Western occult writing into one book, much of it drawn from Renaissance sources like Cornelius Agrippa. It covers natural magic, astrology, talismans and, in this section, the nature of good and evil spirits. Read today, it is less a manual than a snapshot of how educated people once tried to map the invisible.

The idea that runs through it is that the practitioner's moral condition matters more than the ritual. A person who is greedy, angry or deceitful is said to draw the wrong kinds of influence, no matter how carefully they follow instructions. Strip away the supernatural framing and you are left with something most people already know from experience. Who you are shapes what you attract and how things go for you.

I find it useful to read texts like this the way you would read old maps. They are wrong about a lot of the geography, but they tell you a great deal about what people were afraid of, what they hoped for and how they explained forces they could feel but not see. Moods, temptations, sudden clarity, bad luck that seems to follow a person around. Every era has had to name those things somehow.

There is also a practical humility in it. We like to think we have outgrown this kind of thinking, but people still talk about energy in a room, about someone being a bad influence, about a project feeling cursed. The vocabulary changed. The experience it describes did not. -MILLZ

Why it matters

I read old texts like this for perspective: humans have always tried to name the forces that move them.

N° S-04 — Soul · Tolimanu

Tao Te Ching — Rivendale music edition

Core ideas

  • The Tao Te Ching, attributed to Laozi, teaches harmony with the Tao, the natural way.
  • Wu wei, effortless action, means not forcing against the grain.
  • Softness, humility and simplicity outlast rigidity.

The Tao Te Ching is short enough to read in an afternoon and dense enough to keep coming back to for years. It is traditionally attributed to Laozi, and its core teaching is that there is a natural way things move, the Tao, and that most trouble comes from fighting it. Pairing it with music, as this edition does, suits the text. It was never meant to be rushed.

Wu wei, usually translated as effortless action or non-forcing, is the idea people get most wrong. It does not mean doing nothing. It means acting in a way that goes with the grain of a situation instead of against it. A good carpenter does not hack at wood. A good negotiator does not shove. They read what is there and work with it, and the result looks easy from the outside because it is.

The book keeps pointing to water as the model. Water is soft and yields to everything, and over time it wears down stone. Rigid things break. Flexible things last. That shows up everywhere once you look for it, in relationships, in careers, in businesses that adapt versus ones that insist the market is wrong.
